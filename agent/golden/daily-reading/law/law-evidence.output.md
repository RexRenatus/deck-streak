# A bonus for speed

## Today's reading <!-- section:reading -->

Today's cards turn to the first question every piece of evidence must answer before any other
question is reached: does it make a difference to the case at all? That quality is relevance. The
bar for it is set deliberately low, and the court holds a separate tool for the rare item that
clears the bar but would do more harm than good in front of a jury. The reading works through one
scene, so that each part of the test arrives attached to a fact you can picture.

Imagine a courier company that pays its riders a bonus for every delivery they complete in under
twenty minutes. The faster a rider works, the more the rider earns, and the company's own pay sheets
record every bonus it pays. One evening a rider, three deliveries behind and hurrying across town,
cuts through a crowded crossing and strikes a pedestrian who was already halfway over. The
pedestrian breaks a wrist and sues the company, claiming that its rider was riding carelessly and
too fast for the crossing.

At trial the pedestrian offers the rider's pay sheet, which shows that the rider earned a bonus on
almost every delivery that evening, including the one he was making when the collision happened.
The company objects. The pay sheet, it says, records money, not speed. Nobody measured how fast the
rider was going, and a jury that learns the company pays its riders to hurry may decide to punish
the company for the way it runs its business instead of deciding what happened at the crossing.

Keep both halves of that objection in view. The first half is an argument about relevance: the pay
sheet proves nothing about the crossing. The second half is an argument about fairness: even if the
pay sheet proves something, it may prove too little to justify the harm it could do. The analysis
treats these two arguments separately, in that order, and a careful answer does the same.

## Issue <!-- section:issue -->

Whether the pedestrian may show the jury the company's pay sheet, which records a bonus for fast
deliveries, to prove that the rider was riding carelessly at the crossing?

## Rule <!-- section:rule -->

- Evidence is relevant if "it has any tendency to make a fact more or less probable than it would be without the evidence" and "the fact is of consequence in determining the action." [@fre-401]
- "Relevant evidence is admissible" unless another source of law provides otherwise, and "Irrelevant evidence is not admissible." [@fre-402]
- The court "may exclude relevant evidence if its probative value is substantially outweighed by a danger of" unfair prejudice, confusing the issues, misleading the jury, undue delay, wasting time, or needlessly presenting cumulative evidence. [@fre-403]

## Application <!-- section:application -->

Start with relevance, because nothing else matters if the evidence fails it. The test has two
parts. First, the evidence must have some tendency to make a fact more or less probable than it
would be without the evidence [@fre-401]. The pay sheet does not show the rider's speed directly,
but it shows that the rider was paid more for every delivery he finished quickly, and that he had
been earning that bonus all evening. A person who is paid to hurry is somewhat more likely to hurry
than one who is not. That inference is modest, but the test asks only for a tendency, not for
proof, so a modest inference is enough [@fre-401].

Second, the fact the evidence bears on must be of consequence in determining the action
[@fre-401]. The pedestrian's claim turns on whether the rider was riding carelessly when he entered
the crossing, and his speed is part of that question. The pay sheet therefore bears on a fact that
decides the case, and both parts of the test are met: the pay sheet is relevant.

Notice what the company's first argument got wrong. It treated relevance as a question of
sufficiency, as though the pay sheet had to prove the rider's speed on its own. It does not.
Evidence is admitted piece by piece, and no single piece has to carry the whole case. The pay sheet
can sit beside the pedestrian's own account of the collision and a witness's description of the
rider's approach, and together they may persuade the jury even though no one of them would alone.

Once the evidence is relevant, the default is admission. Relevant evidence comes in unless another
source of law keeps it out, while irrelevant evidence never does [@fre-402]. Nothing about a pay
sheet calls for a special exclusion, so the company's remaining hope is the court's power to
exclude relevant evidence whose value is outweighed [@fre-403].

That power has a demanding threshold. The court weighs the evidence's probative value against the
dangers the balancing test lists, and it may exclude the evidence only when its value is
substantially outweighed [@fre-403]. Here the probative value is real but limited: the pay sheet
shows a reason to hurry, not the hurrying itself. The danger the company names is unfair
prejudice, the risk that the jury decides on an improper basis, for example by punishing the
company for a pay scheme it dislikes rather than finding what happened at the crossing. There is
also some risk of confusing the issues, if the trial drifts into a debate about how couriers ought
to be paid.

Those dangers are genuine, but on these facts they do not substantially outweigh the evidence's
value. The pay sheet is short, it takes little time to present, and it speaks to the central
question of the case. The court can also reduce the danger without excluding the evidence: it can
tell the jury that the pay sheet may be used to decide whether the rider was hurrying, and not to
judge whether the company's pay scheme is wise. A court that admitted the pay sheet with that
caution would act well within its discretion, and so would a court that trimmed the parts of the
sheet that concern other riders.

Finally, keep the two arguments in their order. A court that skipped straight to the balance would
never ask whether the evidence cleared the low bar of relevance first, and a court that stopped at
relevance would forget that relevant evidence can still be excluded. The analysis runs relevance,
then the default of admission, then the balance, and only the last step weighs anything.

## Conclusion <!-- section:conclusion -->

The pay sheet is relevant: it gives the rider a reason to hurry, which makes careless riding at the
crossing somewhat more probable, and careless riding is the question the pedestrian's claim turns
on. Relevant evidence is admitted unless something keeps it out, and the company's fear that the
jury will punish its pay scheme does not substantially outweigh the pay sheet's value on these
facts. The court should admit the pay sheet, and it may caution the jury to use it only on the
question of the rider's speed.
