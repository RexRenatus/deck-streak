# The stairwell and the cleaning log

## Today's reading <!-- section:reading -->

Today's twelve new cards gather the three rules that decide whether a jury ever hears a piece of
evidence: the definition of relevance, the general rule that relevant evidence comes in, and the
court's power to keep out relevant evidence that would do more harm than good. With that many cards
the reading takes its time. It follows one dispute from the first objection to the last ruling, and
it stops at every step to name the rule that is doing the work, so that each card has a fact to
hang on.

Picture a small office building whose owner hires a cleaning contractor to sweep and mop the
stairwell every evening. The contractor keeps a log in which each cleaner signs the date and the
floors finished. One winter morning a tenant descends the stairwell, slips on a wet step, and
fractures an ankle. The tenant sues the building's owner, saying that the owner let the stairwell
stay wet and slick and never warned anyone about it.

At trial the tenant offers the contractor's cleaning log. The log shows that on the evening before
the fall the cleaner signed for the third floor and the fourth floor but left the stairwell line
blank. The tenant also offers a second document, a memo written two years earlier in which the owner
grumbles that the contractor costs too much and that the owner is thinking of firing it. The owner
objects to both. Of the log the owner says that a blank line proves nothing, because cleaners forget
to sign all the time. Of the memo the owner says that a complaint about price has nothing to do with
a wet step, and that a jury which reads a landlord grumbling about costs will simply dislike the
landlord. The judge must sort the two objections, and the three rules give her the order in which to
do it.

## Issue <!-- section:issue -->

Whether the tenant may put before the jury both the cleaning log with its blank stairwell line and
the old memo in which the owner complains about the contractor's price, to prove that the owner let
the stairwell stay wet and never warned anyone about it?

## Rule <!-- section:rule -->

Evidence is relevant if "it has any tendency to make a fact more or less probable than it would be without the evidence" and "the fact is of consequence in determining the action." [@fre-401] Relevant evidence is admissible unless the Constitution, a federal statute, the rules themselves or other rules prescribed by the Supreme Court provide otherwise, and "Irrelevant evidence is not admissible." [@fre-402] Even so, the court "may exclude relevant evidence if its probative value is substantially outweighed by a danger of" unfair prejudice, confusing the issues, misleading the jury, undue delay, wasting time, or needlessly presenting cumulative evidence. [@fre-403]

## Application <!-- section:application -->

Begin with the definition, because every other question waits behind it. The test has two parts and
the judge asks them in order. The first part asks whether the evidence has any tendency to make a
fact more or less probable than it would be without the evidence [@fre-401]. The second part asks
whether that fact is of consequence in determining the action [@fre-401]. Neither part asks whether
the evidence is strong, or whether it settles anything. A small nudge toward a fact is a tendency,
and a tendency is all the definition demands.

Take the fact first, because it is the easier half. The tenant's claim is that the owner let the
stairwell stay wet and never warned anyone. Whether the stairwell was in fact left unmopped on the
evening before the fall is a fact of consequence, since the whole action turns on the condition of
the step. That satisfies the second part for the log, and it is worth seeing that the same is true
of the memo only if the memo can be tied to that condition. The judge does not stop at the label
that a fact sounds important. She asks whether the case would come out differently depending on how
the fact is resolved [@fre-401].

Now the tendency. The blank line in the log does not prove that the stairwell was left unmopped. The
owner is right about that, and the point is candid: a cleaner may have finished the stairwell and
merely failed to sign. But the definition does not ask for proof. It asks whether the log makes the
unmopped stairwell somewhat more probable than it would be if the log did not exist [@fre-401].
Consider the world without the log. All the jury knows is that a cleaning contract existed and that
someone was supposed to mop. Consider the world with the log. Now the jury knows that the very
document meant to record the work shows the third and fourth floors done and the stairwell skipped.
A skipped entry on a record that is otherwise filled in is a modest sign that the work was skipped.
Modest is enough, because the rule sets the bar deliberately low [@fre-401]. The log is relevant.

Because it is relevant, the general rule takes over. Relevant evidence is admissible unless the
Constitution, a statute, the rules or other prescribed rules say otherwise [@fre-402]. The owner has
pointed to no such source that bars a business record of this kind, so the log comes in. How much the
log persuades is a matter of weight, and weight belongs to the jury [@fre-402].

The memo is a harder case, and it shows how the three rules divide the work. Ask the tendency
question of the memo. A landlord who grumbles about a contractor's price, two years before the fall,
tells the jury almost nothing about whether a stairwell was mopped on one particular evening. The
tenant will say that an owner who resents paying for cleaning may have been careless about whether
cleaning was done. That is a chain of inference with several weak links: the owner's mood, the
owner's conduct, the contractor's conduct, and the state of a single step. Each link is possible,
and none is strong. A judge could fairly hold that the memo has only a very slight tendency to make
the wet stairwell more probable, or she could hold that the tendency is too thin to count at all. If
it fails the first part of the definition, the memo is irrelevant and it is not admissible, and the
inquiry ends there [@fre-402].

Suppose the judge takes the more generous view and finds that the memo clears the low bar by a hair.
Then the third rule matters, because the general rule of admission is not the last word. The court
may exclude relevant evidence if its probative value is substantially outweighed by a danger of
unfair prejudice, confusing the issues, misleading the jury, undue delay, wasting time, or
needlessly presenting cumulative evidence [@fre-403]. Here the judge weighs two things against each
other. On one side is the memo's probative value, which by hypothesis is slight. On the other side
are the dangers the owner names: that the jury will dislike a landlord who complains about costs and
will punish him for his attitude, and that the trial will drift into a side dispute about the
contractor's fees. A jury that resents the owner's penny-pinching may find against him even if the
stairwell was mopped, and that is the very reaction the rule calls unfair prejudice [@fre-403].

The word substantially carries the weight of the balance. The rule does not let the judge exclude
evidence because the danger is larger than the value. It lets her exclude evidence only when the
value is substantially outweighed by the danger [@fre-403]. The scale is tilted toward admission on
purpose. For the log, the balance is easy: its value is real and its danger is small, since a
jury that learns the log has a blank line will not be inflamed by it. For the memo, the balance
tips the other way, because a slight value set against a real risk of punishing the owner for his
character is the situation the rule was written for. The judge may reasonably exclude the memo and
admit the log, and she can explain each ruling by pointing to a different rule.

## Conclusion <!-- section:conclusion -->

The tenant may show the jury the cleaning log, and the judge should probably keep out the owner's
old memo. The blank stairwell line makes an unmopped stairwell somewhat more probable, and that fact
is of consequence in the tenant's claim, so the log is relevant and admissible. The memo's tendency
to show a wet stairwell is slight at best, and if the judge counts it as relevant its slight value
is substantially outweighed by the danger that the jury will punish the owner for complaining about
cost. The judge answers the issue by running the definition, the general rule and the balance, in
that order.
